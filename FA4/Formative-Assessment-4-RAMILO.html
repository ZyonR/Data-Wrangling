Given your intuition for what growth curves look like, which of these two values of the degrees of freedom makes more sense?

The minimum degree of freedom between df.min and df.1se is the best degrees of freedom, this is because the more the degree of freedom is the more it is overfitted to the data itself and fail to generalize the behavior of the data, which is why a lower degree of freedom is encouraged as it makes itself more generalized such that once new data will arrive, it can predict the outcome of the observation compared to a model that has a higher degree of freedom. However, it is still important to note that to not overly generalize the model to the point that df=1 which might not capture the whole behavior of the data.

This apple season is right around the corner, and you’d like to predict the yield of each tree. You come up with perhaps the simplest possible prediction rule: predict this year’s yield for any given tree based on last year’s yield from that same tree. Without doing any programming, answer the following questions:

  1. What is the training error of such a rule?

The training error will show that it will be non-zero as the yield last year does not necessary imply anything strong on predicting the next yield.

  1. What is the mean squared bias, mean variance, and expected test error of this prediction rule?

The mean square bias would be that the model would low as the data would be trained on a single data point, and for the variance it would be non-zero as well where the same prediction model would not apply to other trees. Lastly for the expected test error would be large given that the data will have different outcomes for each tree.

  1. Why is this not the best possible prediction rule?

This prediction rule is not the best as the data cannot accurately represent real world observations and underlying factors into growing apples. This is also dangerous as it would not be reproducable in a sense that it would not be applied to the other trees within the farm.

As a second attempt to predict a yield for each tree, you average together last year’s yields of the K trees closest to it (including itself, and breaking ties randomly if necessary). So if you choose K = 1, you get back the simple rule from the previous section. This more general rule is called K-nearest neighbors (KNN) regression (see ISLR p. 105).KNN is not a parametric model like linear or logistic regression, so it is a little harder to pin down its degrees of freedom.

  1. What happens to the model complexity as K increases? Why?

As K increases the more time it takes to calculate the values this is because the model needs more time and computing power since the number of trees being included is being increased by one unit.

  1. The degrees of freedom for KNN is sometimes considered n/K, where n is the training set size. Why might this be the case? [Hint: consider a situation where the data are clumped in groups of K.]

  2. Conceptually, why might increasing K tend to improve the prediction rule? What does this have to do with the bias-variance tradeoff?

Conceptually the prediction rule will improve because the data will not be biased to only close neighbors of that tree and will consider more of the surrounding trees.

  1. Conceptually, why might increasing K tend to worsen the prediction rule? What does this have to do with the bias-variance tradeoff?

Conceptually the prediction rule will worsen because as K increases there would be posibilities that there would be outlier trees that will produce more yield or less yeild and would not help in predicting the yield of the single tree we are looking at.
